## A house that breaks the all-white rule

Much of what gets labelled "Scandinavian" online is white walls, white trim, pale wood. This room is a useful correction. The foreground room has sage green walls and a honey-toned wood-plank ceiling; step through the wide white-trimmed doorway and the walls shift to a soft sky blue, under the same warm wood ceiling. The two rooms are clearly connected — same ceiling, same flooring, same white door and window trim — but each has its own colour identity. It's a simple trick: keep the bones consistent, let the wall colour do the work of zoning.

## The dining nook by the window

In the blue room, a bench and chairs are arranged around a table pushed into the corner by two windows, one dressed with a cafe curtain. The tablecloth is a patterned cream rather than a plain one, and the bench cushions and chair seats pick up a warm orange that ties back to the green room next door. A dark rug grounds the seating area against the pale pine floor. A row of small framed artworks is hung above a shelf on the window wall at seated eye height — a nice, low-key way to add art without competing with the windows for light.

## A pendant doing almost nothing, and doing it well

The single white ceramic pendant hanging in the middle of the blue room is unfussy — a simple dome shape, bare bulb glow, nothing decorative about it. Against the wood ceiling and blue walls it doesn't need to be a statement piece; the room already has enough personality from colour and pattern. It's a good reminder that not every light fixture has to be the focal point.

## The bookcase wall and the foosball table

Further into the blue room, a built-in-style wooden bookcase runs floor to ceiling, packed tightly with books in a way that reads as genuinely lived-in rather than styled. Below it, a low wooden sideboard holds a few objects and a bag hangs off the end. In front of all this sits a foosball table — not hidden away, not apologised for, just part of the room. It's a good example of a Scandinavian home prioritising how a family actually uses a space over how a room photographs for a catalogue.

## The green room as entry and plant corner

Back in the foreground, the green room functions as an in-between space — an opening with a large framed moon lithograph lettered LUNE on the wall, a woven plant stand holding several trailing and upright plants, and more small framed art on the right wall. It's a transitional zone, and the plants and prints keep it from feeling like empty circulation space.

## Interior tips

- **Let the ceiling material travel between rooms.** The same wood-plank ceiling running through both the green and blue rooms is what makes two bold, different wall colours feel like one coherent home rather than two clashing rooms.
- **Use a patterned textile to bridge two colour zones.** The orange cushion fabric in the blue dining nook echoes the warmth of the green room next door, so the eye reads them as part of the same palette.
- **Keep statement-colour rooms simple on lighting.** A plain dome pendant works harder in a neutral room; here, with blue walls and a busy bookcase, a quiet fixture is the right call.
- **Don't hide the fun furniture.** A foosball table in the main living space, left in full view rather than tucked into a basement, signals a home built around use rather than show.
- **Group small art at seated height.** The cluster of framed pieces above the window-side bench sits low and close together, which works better at dining-table eye level than a single large piece higher up the wall.
- **Let a plant stand do double duty as a room's threshold marker.** The woven plant stand by the entry visually separates the green room from the hallway without adding a physical divider.
